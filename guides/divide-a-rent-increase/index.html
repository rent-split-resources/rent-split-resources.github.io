How roommates can divide a rent increase fairly

A rent increase changes the total, but it does not have to restart every discussion from scratch. First check what your current agreement says, then compare the available methods.

THE STARTING POINT

Keeping existing percentages preserves the relative room prices. Splitting the increase equally preserves the dollar gaps. Choose deliberately.

1. Confirm the new rent and effective date

Check the written notice, the amount, and the date the change starts. Notice periods, rent limits, and renewal rules depend on the lease and local law. If the notice is unclear or disputed, verify it before treating the new figure as settled.

Work with the actual increase: new rent − old rent. Keep changes to utilities or other charges separate so you can see what is changing.

2. Preserve existing percentages

Suppose rent rises from $2,400 to $2,550, an increase of $150. Three roommates currently pay $960, $840, and $600: shares of 40%, 35%, and 25%.

Keeping those percentages gives new payments of $1,020, $892.50, and $637.50. The increases are $60, $52.50, and $37.50. This method preserves the relative pricing of rooms and can fit an arrangement originally based on room differences.

New share = old share ÷ old total rent × new total rent

3. Compare an equal-dollar increase

Alternatively, divide the $150 increase equally: $50 per person. New payments become $1,010, $890, and $650. They still add to $2,550, but the percentages change.

An equal-dollar increase keeps the existing dollar differences between roommates. Neither approach is automatically correct for every home; use the agreement and the reasons behind the original split.

4. Recalculate if the household has changed

If someone moved into a larger room, a partner moved in, or a parking space changed hands, simply scaling old shares may preserve an outdated arrangement. Revisit the original rent-splitting method with the new facts.

Confirm each amount, the first payment date, and whether any standing transfer needs updating. Record the revised agreement and check that the payments add to the new rent. A household decision about the split does not change the landlord's rights or the tenants' obligations under the lease.

Common questions

Does the biggest room always take the biggest increase?

It does when you preserve existing percentages and that room already has the largest share. An equal-dollar increase assigns the same extra amount to each roommate.

Should we renegotiate every time rent changes?

Review the existing agreement first. If rooms, occupants, and the reasons for the original split are unchanged, reusing the agreed method may be sufficient.
