How to split rent fairly between roommates

A fair rent split starts with an agreement about what each person is paying for. Equal shares can work well, but a bigger bedroom, a private bathroom, or a couple sharing one room can change the conversation.

THE STARTING POINT

Choose the rule together, run the numbers, and write the agreement down before anyone commits to a room.

1. Choose a method that fits your home

Start by listing the monthly rent, the people living in the home, and the differences between bedrooms. Compare room size, natural light, storage, bathrooms, parking, and noise. A formula helps make an agreement clear; it cannot decide what everyone values.

MethodHow it worksWhen it fits
Equal splitTotal rent ÷ number of peopleSimilar rooms and similar access

2. Try an equal split first

For a $2,400 apartment with three roommates, an equal share is $2,400 ÷ 3 = $800 per person. If shared utilities are $180, each adds $60, giving a total of $860 per person.

Keep rent and utilities as separate lines. Rent is usually fixed for the rental period; utilities can vary and may need a different allocation. A person working from home does not automatically owe more: agree on any usage adjustment together.

Monthly contribution = rent share + utilities share

Use the rent split calculator to find each person’s equal share of the monthly rent.

3. Account for differences between rooms

If one bedroom is substantially larger, choose how much of the rent represents shared access and how much represents private space. For example, you might share 40% equally and divide 60% by bedroom area. The 40% is an agreed assumption, not a measured fact about the property's value.

A private bathroom or parking space can instead have an agreed monthly premium. If one roommate pays $100 more than an equal share, reduce the others' combined shares by $100. A premium must redistribute the fixed total rather than increase it.

See the room-size formula and worked example. For two people in one room, use the couples and shared rooms guide.

4. Use income only with everyone's agreement

An income-based split can suit a household that wants contributions to reflect affordability. If two people earn $3,000 and $2,000 a month, their shares of combined income are 60% and 40%. On $1,500 rent, they would pay $900 and $600.

Agree whether the income figure means take-home pay, how to handle variable income, and when to review it. Sharing income information should be voluntary. This arrangement is a household choice, not a rule that every roommate must accept.

5. Write a short roommate agreement

  • Record each person's rent share and the method behind it.
  • List which bills are shared and who pays each provider.
  • Set a payment date that leaves time before rent is due.
  • Agree on deposits, refunds, guests, and partial months separately.
  • Choose review triggers: a rent increase, a move-out, or a room change.

A household agreement may not change what the lease requires. Read the lease and check local rules if responsibilities are unclear. Revisit the arrangement when the facts change rather than assuming the first split will always fit.

Common questions

Does fair always mean equal?

No. Equal is simple, but differences in private space or an agreed affordability arrangement can justify different shares.

Should utilities use the same percentages as rent?

They can, but they do not have to. An equal per-person utility split is often easier when usage is similar, even when bedrooms have different prices.
