How to split rent by room size

A larger bedroom can reasonably cost more without making the whole rent depend on bedroom size. Separating shared access from private space gives you a clear starting point.

THE STARTING POINT

Share an agreed portion equally, then allocate the remaining rent by each bedroom's share of total private room area.

1. Measure the private rooms consistently

Measure the usable floor area of each bedroom using the same unit. For a rectangular room, multiply length by width. A room measuring 12 feet by 10 feet has an area of 120 square feet. You can use square metres instead; only the ratios matter.

Decide together whether to include a private closet or bathroom. Do not mix square feet and square metres. Shared kitchens, living rooms, and hallways belong in the shared portion, not in one roommate's bedroom measurement.

2. Apply the two-part formula

Let R be total rent, c the fraction allocated to shared access, n the number of roommates, Aᵢ one roommate's room area, and A the sum of all bedroom areas. With one person per room:

Rent shareᵢ = (R × c ÷ n) + (R × (1 − c) × Aᵢ ÷ A)

The fraction c is a negotiated assumption. For example, c = 0.40 means 40% of rent is divided equally. It does not claim that common spaces are worth exactly 40% of market rent. A larger shared portion brings the shares closer together.

3. Work through a $2,400 example

Three roommates have bedrooms measuring 120, 100, and 80 square feet. Total bedroom area is 300 square feet. They agree to split 40% of rent equally and 60% by room size.

The shared portion is $960, or $320 each. The private portion is $1,440, allocated in the ratios 120/300, 100/300, and 80/300.

4. Adjust for features a measurement misses

Area does not capture everything. A quiet room, en suite bathroom, sunny balcony, or reserved parking can matter more than a few square feet. Discuss any fixed adjustment after calculating the baseline and make sure the adjusted shares still add to the total.

For a couple sharing a bedroom, do not assign the entire room's area to each partner; that double-counts private space. Allocate the private portion once per room, then divide that room's cost between its occupants. The shared-room guide shows how.

5. Resolve the last cent

A calculation can produce fractions of a cent. Round down each share to whole cents, then assign the remaining cents to the largest fractional remainders. If remainders tie, use a consistent order and rotate it over time if desired.

For example, $1,000 divided equally among three people becomes $333.34, $333.33, and $333.33. This is more useful than rounding every share to $333.33 and leaving the household short by a cent. Our calculator reconciles the total automatically.

Common questions

What shared-space percentage should we use?

There is no universal correct percentage. Agree on a starting point and compare the resulting room prices. This guide uses 40% as an example; your household can agree on a different percentage.

Can I use square metres?

Yes. Use the same unit for every room; the calculation uses proportions, so either square metres or square feet works.
